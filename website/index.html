About

925law is me: Max Englhard, attorney-at-law.

My career hasn’t run in a straight line: private practice, a fintech, the public sector, and most recently a large commercial firm. I’ve seen a lot and come to know both sides. I know what matters to a business, and just as well how it feels to be an employee.

What drives me is simple: I like helping people, and law is how I do it. Most of my clients aren’t interested in legal fine print. They want a solution. That is what I’m good at: working out quickly where the real problem lies and finding a route through it that works for you. And I explain it in a way that makes sense without a law degree.

When things get serious

You may recognise yourself in one of these.

  • Dismissal

    You’ve been handed a dismissal or a termination agreement. I work out a strategy with you, challenge the dismissal and negotiate your severance. You have three weeks from receiving the notice to file a claim. Miss that deadline and, as a rule, the dismissal stands, even if it was unlawful.

  • Contracts

    You need a contract that holds up when it matters, or you have one that gives you a bad feeling. I draft it so it protects your interests and isn’t the first thing to buckle in a dispute.

  • Job reference

    Your job reference is worse than your work was, and it could cost you your next job. I read between the lines of the coded phrasing, tell you what it really says and press for an assessment that reflects your work, in court if need be.

  • Unpaid claims

    A client isn’t paying, and reminders stopped working long ago. I pursue your claim firmly, out of court and, if needed, in court.

  • Disciplinary proceedings

    You’re facing disciplinary proceedings, with your career on the line. I examine every allegation and every procedural step, and defend you with everything the procedure allows.

  • Letting someone go

    You have to let an employee go and want to keep the risk low. I plan the separation with you, from the grounds to the wording, so it gives a court as little as possible to pick apart and you know the likely cost in advance.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

  1. How much does a first consultation cost?
    The first consultation is a flat €226.10 including VAT (€190 net). We go through your situation, I assess your prospects and the risks, and I explain the next steps and what they are likely to cost. If you then instruct me, the consultation fee is credited in full against my further fees.

  2. I’ve been dismissed. Is there a deadline?
    Yes. If you want to challenge the dismissal, your claim must reach the labour court within three weeks of receiving the notice (section 4 of the Dismissal Protection Act, KSchG). If you miss that deadline, the dismissal will as a rule be treated as valid, even if it was not. You should also register as a jobseeker with the Federal Employment Agency within three days of learning when your employment ends; if that date is more than three months away, registering at least three months before it is enough. This applies even if you go to court; otherwise you risk a suspension of unemployment benefit (section 38 SGB III). And do not sign anything until it has been checked.

  3. Am I entitled to severance pay?
    German law gives no general right to severance pay. A claim can arise from a redundancy scheme agreed with the works council (Sozialplan), a collective agreement or your employment contract. It can also arise under section 1a KSchG, where the employer dismisses for operational reasons, states in the dismissal letter that you can claim severance if you let the deadline for bringing a claim pass, and you then do not go to court. In that case the severance is half a month’s pay for each year of employment. More often, severance is negotiated, frequently alongside a dismissal claim, and how much is realistic depends largely on how vulnerable the dismissal is.

  4. Who pays the costs in a labour court case?
    At first instance, each side pays its own lawyer, whatever the outcome (section 12a of the Labour Courts Act, ArbGG). If you lose, you do not pay the other side’s legal fees, but if you win, you cannot recover your own. This applies to employers and employees alike. Court fees are comparatively low and fall away entirely if the whole case ends in a court settlement.

  5. Should I have my freelance contract reviewed?
    In framework and project contracts, the risk usually sits in a handful of clauses: liability, payment terms, rights of use, termination and penalty clauses. I review the contract, point out what could cause you problems and suggest wording you can negotiate. I can also handle the negotiation for you. I tell you what the work will cost before I start.

  6. Can I get advice in English?
    Yes. I advise in English and German, including on English-language contracts governed by German law. Just get in touch in whichever language you prefer.

  7. How quickly will I hear back?
    I usually reply to enquiries sent through the contact form within one business day. If a deadline is running, for example after a dismissal, please say so in your message and include the date you received the letter, so I can schedule the consultation with that in mind.
